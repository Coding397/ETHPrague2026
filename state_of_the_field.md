> *Card heading:* Web3 Privacy Now, 17:00 - 22:00 CEST

> *Sylvia:* Arrive at ten to five to find it is all a bit not ready yet. A young man tells us that they are starting at 5:30 but want to give people a chance to mingle. A Czech man enters after me and asks the young man something. The young man does not speak Czech. The oolder man tries again, in halting English, asking where the seminary is. "Not here," says the young man. "I don't know." He looks around, spots an escape opportunity. "Sorry." And he's gone.
>
> I try in German and the man responds. Is he here for teh Web3Privacy now event? He's here for Web3, he tells me. But he he doesn't understand what it is. "The talks (I'm guessing from Seminary) are in there," I say, pointing at a room full of empty chairs. "Oh, so it's a spoken event," he asks? "Yes, mostly. It will start at 5:30." "You know more than him." He points at the young man with light brown hair to his shoulders and a black t-shirt that says Web3privacynow in small print. Black t-shirt pretends not to notice. "He knows nothing." He walks into the room of chairs, looks around, walks out again. Speaks to someone else, who apparently also knows nothing, then speaks to me in broken English, having forgotten (or perhaps not noticed) that we were speaking German. He wants to know when it is starting, and I'm now the only person he trusts with questions, apparently, although my only claimto fame is that I was listening when black tshirt explained the drill.

> *Sylvia:* The music playing best of Dizzee Rascal

> *Card heading:* Leaving

> *Sylvia:* The issue is that there are uncomfortable chairs in an assembly hall where the lighting is failing. There are no lights on the people. I'm happy with an informal situation where we sit comfortably and talk to people, and I get to listen to people who are smarter than me. I'm also open to a conference-style presentation being given with slides, with the audience and the speaker. This was the worst of both worlds. It was seriously informal and all very quickly put together, seemingly. What we ended up with was people sitting there in uncomfortable chairs watching people speak as it got dark.

> *Sylvia:* The point is that if you're going to do that kind of presentation, I think there needs to be a certain level of professionalism. Everything, ranging from the way the speakers were named by nickname only, which made it very difficult to make sense of when the person then just stood up and was introduced by their name, is like "who's who?" Now, for example, Mario Havel stood up and spoke until the end of his speech. It was not clear that TMI Chell was the same person. I understand that within the community these people are known and thus they don't need that much introduction, but within the context of a Prague meetup, it would have been nice, tying in with a European conference, if there had been some stronger attempt to introduce the new people to the speakers: who they were, where they came from, why they were speaking to us, and why they were worth listening to. Without this, I think a lot of people lost concentration as they try to work out which talks were actually relevant to them.

> *Sylvia:* It was very noticeable that, as the evening went on, every speaker had fewer and fewer people in the audience. That was actually, I think, unrelated to their subjects and only a case of attrition, because the people who were interested in hearing people speak sat down at the start. One by one, they became unable to concentrate or focus or had just heard enough. The point is that there were no new people coming in. There was no information in the main room as to who was speaking and what about. There was actually no push for anyone who was in the main room to come into the auditorium room once they had left, or especially not if they'd never been there in the first place.

> *Sylvia:* The social aspect of it was good. It worked very well. There are people sitting in the back, sitting at tables at the back in the courtyard out back. The rooms were full of people talking. In fact, that was probably part of the problem: while we sat in an auditorium-style room full of uncomfortable chairs, the sound of people talking and drinking beer was a little bit hard to ignore.

> *Sylvia:* The seats went straight across the room, which meant that the only route to get into a seat that wasn't the back row was to go down the right side of the room and then to shuffle past the people who were sitting there. There was no middle aisle. There was no way to get up and down the left side of the room. The result is that once you left, there really was a barrier to coming back, because you have to negotiate that again. Considering that the seats were never more than one quarter full, taking away the central row of seats would have made a big difference.

> *Sylvia:* I felt sorry for the people who were later in the line-up as the crowd dwindled. There was still an hour to go when I picked myself up and walked out, actually just really needing to stretch from having been sitting down. Each of the speeches, each of the presentations, was interesting. There was a lot to chew on, but the practicalities of it let it down.

> **The setup:**
>
> A campaign has four parameters:
> - **G** = the funding goal
> - **T** = the deadline
> - **b** = donor benefit per unit (what a donor gets in return — like a Kickstarter perk)
> - **β** (beta) = drafted-bet benefit fraction (how much of that perk a *bettor* gets if their bet gets "drafted" into being a donation)
>
> **Three things people can do, all irrevocable:**
>
> - **Donate** — you contribute money, you get benefit *b* if the campaign succeeds. Standard Kickstarter.
> - **YES bet** — you bet that the campaign will hit its goal by the deadline.
> - **NO bet** — you bet it won't. If your NO bet ends up "drafted" (converted into part of the funding), you get a fraction β of the donor benefit.
>
> **The phases:**
>
> - **Pre-lock**: Donations + YES bets + NO bets together are still less than the goal. Everything is open, everyone can do anything.
> - **Lock**: Once the combined total (D + Y + N) reaches the goal, bets *freeze*. Because everything's irrevocable, success is now guaranteed by the maths — even the NO bets are effectively committed money that helps reach the goal.
> - **Post-lock**: Only donations are accepted. Late donations "crowd out" the drafted bets — meaning if real donations come in, they replace the bet money in the funding stack until donations alone clear the goal.
>
> **The clever bit (from your notes):**
>
> The two bet pools are *each other's* reward pool. NOes only show up if there are YESes to bet against. YESes only show up if there are NOes. They're symbiotic.
>

Once enough NO money is in there, a tipping point: hitting the goal becomes more likely (because that bet capital itself counts toward the total), so YES bets become attractive, more capital flows in, and you close on the goal. **The bets don't just predict the outcome — they cause it.**
>
> That's the title: "When Bets Can Influence Outcomes." Prediction markets that fund the thing they're predicting.
>

>

> The two things storage actually does

> The two primitives (node operator side)

> The "reframe" slide is the punchline:
> Bandwidth side: zero-cash entry, no throttling, continuous access regardless of whether you have money on hand. Like reading a library book — free at the point of use.

> The whole arc
> Storage isn't one thing → it's two commodities (bandwidth-now vs. disk-over-time) → which map to two node-operator roles (cache vs. reserve) → which map to two user-facing products (free upload vs. paid retention) → which gives users a guarantee of outcome on both axes.

> - *Single*: you run one node, you pin your own stuff. Like keeping a file on your own hard drive.
> - *Cluster*: a few people you trust pool their nodes together. Like a shared family hard drive.
> - *DSN* (Decentralised Service Network): a public network of strangers pin it for you, anchored by economic guarantees. Like the postal system — you don't know who handles your letter, but the system works.
>
> Same hammer, different jobs.
>
> **Cold storage / scan & repair**: For the archival tier, you don't keep a million copies of everything (that's wasteful). Instead, the network periodically *checks* whether data is still there and intact, and *repairs* (rebuilds) only what's degraded — using the erasure-coding maths from earlier. > - *Price = cost + risk. Yield = the spread.*
